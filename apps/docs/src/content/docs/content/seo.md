---
title: Search engines and social cards
description: The SEO panel, what it falls back to, and why the length warnings are only warnings.
---

The **Search engines** panel sits in the item editor's sidebar, below Publishing. Every field in it
is optional.

## Meta title

What a search engine shows as the clickable headline, and what a browser tab says.

Leave it blank and the item's own title is used. Fill it in when the page's title works on the page
but not out of context — "Apply" is fine as a heading under Admissions and useless as a search
result. "Apply to Riverbend College" is not.

## Meta description

The sentence or two beneath the headline in search results.

**There is deliberately no fallback here.** If you leave it blank, Taproot sends nothing and the
search engine picks its own snippet from the page — which is very often better than anything
automatic. Taproot could truncate the first paragraph, and it reads exactly like what it is: a
machine cutting a sentence in half.

Write one when you have something specific to say. Leaving it blank is a legitimate choice, not an
unfinished field.

## Social image

The picture shown when the page is shared on social media or in a chat app.

Falls back to the content type's default social image if one is set, so most pages need nothing
here. Set it per item for a page that deserves its own.

## Hide from search engines

Asks search engines not to index the page. Good for a thank-you page or a form landing.

It is a request, not a barrier. The page is still public and still reachable by anyone with the
address. It is not a way to keep something private.

## The length warnings

Past roughly 60 characters for a title or 160 for a description, Taproot warns you — and still saves
exactly what you wrote.

They are warnings rather than limits because no character count is actually right: search engines
truncate by pixel width, so thirty capital Ws and thirty lowercase i's are not the same length. The
numbers are a useful signal and a bad rule.

## The preview

The panel shows how the page is likely to appear, with the fallbacks already applied — so you see
what a visitor sees rather than what you typed.

Those fallbacks are worked out in the same place the live page works them out. A preview that
resolved its own would be a preview of a page nobody will ever see, and you would not find out for
weeks.
